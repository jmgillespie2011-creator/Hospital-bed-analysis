Where the beds went: England's hospitals at 92% full

England's hospitals have fewer general and acute beds than at almost any time on record. They also serve a population that is larger and much older than it was. We put three decades of published NHS data together with a simulation of every emergency and planned admission. The aim was to show what fullness costs, why that cost rose sharply after the pandemic, and which policies would actually free up space.

2. Full hospitals, and the break after the pandemic

A full hospital makes patients wait. When no bed is free, the patient stays in A&E, sometimes on a trolley in a corridor, until one is. NHS England publishes the number who wait more than 12 hours from the decision to admit them. Before 2020 that was a rare event. Today it happens to about of emergency admissions.

The striking thing is not that waits rise as hospitals fill. It is that the same occupancy now produces far longer waits than it did before the pandemic. Each dot below is one quarter. Before 2020, quarters at 90–93% occupancy had almost no 12-hour waits (under 0.3% of admissions). Since 2022, quarters at the same occupancy have had 5–11% of admissions waiting more than 12 hours.

Why? No published study has tested it. Patients are older and sicker, and more of them are waiting to be discharged. Beds may also be less interchangeable than before, through more single-specialty wards, ring-fenced planned-surgery beds and infection-control rules. The simulator can only reproduce today's waits if hospitals behave as if their beds are split into small pools that cannot lend to each other, and the waits of 2018/19 only if the pools are large. We show both versions throughout, as "today's hospitals" and "pre-pandemic flexibility".

We tested whether something other than flexibility could explain the change. Give the model 2018/19's own beds, admissions and occupancy, with today's flexibility. Removing discharge delays entirely makes almost no difference once occupancy is matched. Then set everything else that matters as favourably as is plausible: steadier lengths of stay, 7-day discharging, no flu season and double today's corridor spaces. It still predicts of emergency admissions waiting over 12 hours, against observed. Matching 2018/19 needs bed pools times larger than today's. Average stays barely changed, so slower turnover overall is not the answer either.

Part of that break is in how the waits are counted. The 12-hour figure starts when a doctor decides to admit. Counted from arrival instead, patients spent over 12 hours in A&E in 2018/19, against counted from the decision to admit. From arrival, 12-hour waits rose about -fold since then; from the decision to admit, about -fold. Measures that do not depend on when a decision is recorded, such as 4-hour waits and ambulance handover delays, rose 2.5 to 4 times at the same occupancy. So hospitals did lose flexibility, but much less than the 12-hour figure alone suggests. The long-stay clue

Comparing hospitals gives a clue. We followed acute trusts through three years before the pandemic and three after. Bigger trusts did not hold up better, which simple pooling theory would predict. What mattered was how many of a trust's beds were held by patients who had been in hospital for three weeks or more. Group the trusts into fifths by that share and the pattern is plain. Before the pandemic, every group had almost no 12-hour waits. Afterwards, trusts in the fifth with the fewest long-stay patients had of emergency admissions waiting over 12 hours. In the fifth with the most, it was , even though occupancy was about the same ().

A statistical model that compares each trust with itself over time, and allows for year-to-year changes in its occupancy, puts the effect at more long waits for each standard deviation of long-stay share. Before the pandemic the same comparison shows nothing ().

Why would long stays only matter after 2020? We tested four ideas.

  • Did long stays simply grow? Not much. The winter sitreps of 2017/18 to 2019/20 show of beds held by patients in hospital three weeks or more, against since. The same trusts tended to have high shares in both periods. Even measured at the time, long stays did not predict long waits before the pandemic (). After it, both a trust's earlier level of long stays () and its rise since () predict the increase in waits.
  • Is it really delayed discharge? No. Patients who no longer meet the criteria to reside do not predict the rise (). The long-stay result also holds when we allow each region its own change ().
  • Does one cause the other? Month by month within a trust, months with more long-stay patients also have more long waits ( for each extra point of long-stay share). But last month's long stays do not predict this month's waits (), and earlier waits do not predict later long stays. The two move together but neither leads.
  • Do long-stay patients block the beds that emergency patients need? We built a separate simulation of a hospital split into 20 specialty wards. With beds fully shared between wards, almost nobody waits 12 hours (). When patients can rarely be placed outside their own ward, waits reach today's level. So losing the ability to place patients flexibly is enough, on its own, to explain the national break. At today's level of flexibility, raising long-stay patients from , and it makes no difference whether they are spread out or concentrated on a few wards.

So the clue is real, but blocked beds are probably not the explanation. A high long-stay share more likely marks hospitals whose patients or local services, such as older and frailer populations or weaker community and social care, struggle most once beds can no longer be shared flexibly. It may also share a common cause with long waits. Getting long-stay patients home matters for those patients, but on this evidence it is not a hidden lever that the simulator misses.

Two cautions. These are associations, not proof of cause. And the trust-level result weakens when the ten trusts with the most long waits are left out (). The validation page has the full tests.

4. Where else could the space come from?

Beds are not the only lever. Fewer people needing admission, shorter stays and faster discharges all free up space. We ran each policy at a size that has been proposed or achieved somewhere. The effect of each on admissions and length of stay comes from measured data, not assumption: OHID attributable-admission estimates for smoking, a large cohort study for obesity, a test-negative study for flu vaccine effectiveness, NHS England's discharge data for delays, and HES readmission counts for readmissions (sources). 6. Not every hospital is the same

England's average hides a wide spread. Across acute trusts in , trusts at the same occupancy had very different long waits. Occupancy matters, but it is far from the whole story: case mix, local social care, A&E design and how wards are run all play a part.

7. The next ten years

What this analysis can and cannot tell you

  • Occupancy is robust. It follows mostly from the arithmetic of admissions, length of stay and beds, and both versions of the model agree.
  • Waits are uncertain. They depend on how flexibly beds can be used, which changed after the pandemic and is not directly measured. Read the two systems as a range.
  • Changes are more reliable than levels. Every scenario uses the same simulated patients as the baseline, so differences reflect the policy, not chance.
  • Demand responds to supply. More beds may let hospitals admit patients they now treat elsewhere, using up some of the gain.
  • The model fails some tests. It does not reproduce the weekly pattern of occupancy, or why some trusts wait so much longer than others. These failures are published on the validation page.
