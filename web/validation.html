Validation

A model is only useful if it reproduces what actually happened. These checks compare the model with observed data, including years it was not calibrated to. Failures are shown as plainly as successes.

The most important finding. One version of the model cannot reproduce long waits both before and after the pandemic. Calibrated to 2025/26, the model predicts several percent of emergency admissions waiting over 12 hours in 2018/19 and 2019/20, when almost none did. A version with much more flexible use of beds matches the pre-pandemic years but predicts almost no long waits today. Hospitals now produce far longer waits at the same occupancy than before the pandemic, consistent with the Royal College of Emergency Medicine's view that hospitals reorganised beds for infection control and now hit capacity problems at lower occupancy (RCEM, 2021). That view has not been tested in published research, and the model's "flexibility" absorbs anything that lengthens waits at a given occupancy, not only bed sharing: see the tests below. Occupancy results barely differ between the two versions; waits differ a lot, so the simulator shows both and the truth for a future scenario is likely to lie between them.

Corridor care

Since May 2026 NHS England has published two counts of corridor care, defined as care for 45 minutes or more in a clinically inappropriate place (source; experimental statistics). The first is ward corridor care: patients on a general & acute ward but outside a bed space, counted at 08:00. This corresponds to the model's temporary escalation spaces, which are used only when every proper bed in a pool is full. The number of those spaces is fitted so the model matches the average count. The pattern by day of week and by month was not fitted, which makes it a test. The second is corridor care in emergency departments: patients counted over each day. This includes people who are never admitted and leaves out those waiting in a proper cubicle. The model has no direct equivalent, so we report the number of admitted patients whose wait for a bed reached 45 minutes alongside it, without a pass mark.

Is it really bed flexibility? Two tests

The model reproduces today's long waits only if beds behave as if split into small pools, and pre-pandemic waits only if the pools are large. "Bed flexibility" is the model's name for whatever lengthens waits at a given occupancy. These two tests ask whether something else could explain the change, and which trusts changed most.

1. Can pre-pandemic years be fitted with today's flexibility?

Each year is run with its own beds, emergency admissions and population, with length of stay re-fitted so occupancy matches that year's KH03 figure. Discharge delays and corridor spaces before 2020 were not measured on today's definitions, so they are varied over a wide range. Factors that matter at a fixed occupancy are also set as favourably as is plausible, one at a time and all together (scripts/flexibility_check.mjs).

2. Which trusts saw the biggest rise in long waits at the same occupancy?

A panel of acute trusts for three years before the pandemic (2017/18 to 2019/20) and three after (2022/23, 2023/24, 2025/26). It uses each trust's KH03 occupancy and its A&E counts of emergency admissions and 12-hour waits from decision to admit. A Poisson regression with trust and year fixed effects asks whether, at the same occupancy, long waits rose more after the pandemic in trusts with a given characteristic. Effects are per standard deviation of the characteristic, with 95% confidence intervals clustered by trust (scripts/trust_flexibility.py).

3. Why did long stays only matter after the pandemic?

Four follow-up tests (scripts/longstay_tests.py and scripts/longstay_mechanism.mjs). A uses the monthly bed sitrep (August 2023 onwards) to compare each trust with itself month by month. B measures long stays before the pandemic, from the winter daily sitreps of 2017/18 to 2019/20. C checks competing explanations: patients no longer meeting the criteria to reside, and regional differences. D is a separate simulation of a hospital split into 20 specialty wards, testing whether long-stay patients blocking particular beds can produce the pattern.

Can area data measure the effect of public-health policies?

Estimates from scripts/effects_analysis.py: how emergency admission rates vary with risk factors across ~150 local authorities (between areas, adjusted for deprivation) and within areas over time (area and year fixed effects). Placebo outcomes (falls, hip fracture) should show no effect of flu vaccination; they do, which shows these designs pick up confounding. The simulator therefore uses OHID's attributable-admission estimates and published studies by default (see Data & sources).

What this means for using the simulator

  • Occupancy estimates are robust: they follow mostly from the arithmetic of demand and beds, and both versions of the model agree.
  • Waits for a bed are sensitive to how flexibly beds can be used. Treat the two systems as a range, not a precise forecast.
  • Direction and size of changes (scenario minus baseline) are more reliable than absolute levels, because most model errors affect the baseline and the scenario alike.
