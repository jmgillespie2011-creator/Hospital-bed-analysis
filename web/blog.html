Where the beds went: England's hospitals at 92% full

England's hospitals have fewer general and acute beds than at almost any time on record. They also serve a population that is larger and much older than it was. We put three decades of published NHS data together with a simulation of every emergency and planned admission. The aim was to show what fullness costs, why that cost rose sharply after the pandemic, who is to blame (social care, GPs or the way hospitals run), and which policies would actually free up space.

2. Full hospitals, and the break after the pandemic

A full hospital makes patients wait. When no bed is free, the patient stays in A&E, sometimes on a trolley in a corridor, until one is. NHS England publishes the number who wait more than 12 hours from the decision to admit them. Before 2020 that was a rare event. Today it happens to about of emergency admissions.

The striking thing is not that waits rise as hospitals fill. It is that the same occupancy now produces far longer waits than it did before the pandemic. Each dot below is one quarter. Before 2020, quarters at 90–93% occupancy had almost no 12-hour waits from the decision to admit (under 0.3% of admissions). Since 2022, quarters at the same occupancy have had 5–11%.

That figure exaggerates the change, because the 12-hour clock starts when a doctor decides to admit, and before the pandemic that decision was often recorded late. Counted from arrival in A&E, patients spent over 12 hours there in 2018/19, against counted from the decision to admit. From arrival, 12-hour waits have risen about -fold since then, not -fold. Four-hour waits and ambulance handover delays, which do not depend on when a decision is recorded, rose 2.5 to 4 times at the same occupancy. The real change is a several-fold rise in long waits at the same level of fullness, and the simulator's pre-pandemic setting is fitted to that, not to the decision-to-admit figure.

Why did it happen? No published study has tested it. Patients are older and sicker, and more of them are waiting to be discharged. Beds may also be less interchangeable than before, through more single-specialty wards, ring-fenced planned-surgery beds and infection-control rules. The simulator reproduces today's waits only if hospitals behave as if their beds are split into small pools, about beds each, that cannot lend to each other. Trusts with many long-stay patients got worse

The rise in long waits was concentrated in trusts with many long-stay patients, but those patients seem to mark strain rather than cause it. We followed acute trusts through three years before the pandemic and three after, grouped into fifths by the share of their beds held by patients in hospital three weeks or more. Before the pandemic, every group had almost no 12-hour waits. Afterwards, the fifth with the fewest long-stay patients had of emergency admissions waiting over 12 hours and the fifth with the most had , at about the same occupancy (). Bigger trusts did not hold up better.

Comparing each trust with itself over time, and allowing for changes in its occupancy, each standard deviation of long-stay share went with more long waits after the pandemic, and with nothing before it (). Four findings point to long stays as a marker rather than the mechanism:

  • Long stays grew only a little. The winter sitreps of 2017/18 to 2019/20 show of beds held by patients in hospital three weeks or more, against since, and the same trusts had high shares in both periods. Before the pandemic, long stays did not predict long waits (); after it, both a trust's earlier level () and its rise since () predict the increase in waits.
  • The total number of patients fit to leave does not predict which trusts got worse (); section 3 shows which kinds of delay do track waits. The long-stay result holds when each region has its own change ().
  • Long stays and long waits move together, but neither leads. Months with more long-stay patients have more long waits ( for each extra point of long-stay share), but last month's long stays do not predict this month's waits (), and earlier waits do not predict later long stays.
  • Blocked beds are too small an effect. In a separate simulation of a hospital split into 20 specialty wards, waits stay near zero when beds are fully shared () and reach today's level when patients can rarely be placed outside their own ward. A high long-stay share more likely marks hospitals whose patients or local services (older and frailer populations, weaker community and social care) struggle most once beds cannot be shared flexibly, or it shares a common cause with long waits. These are associations, and the trust-level result weakens when the ten trusts with the most long waits are left out (). The validation page has the full tests.

3. Who is to blame? Social care, GPs, or how hospitals run

Three explanations dominate the debate. Social care cannot take patients who are ready to leave, so they block beds. GPs are too hard to see, so patients end up in A&E and then in hospital. Or hospitals themselves manage the flow of patients badly. We tested each against published data, then asked the simulator how much each could matter. None of these tests proves cause on its own, and each has gaps we point out, but together they point one way.

5. Where else could the space come from?

Beds are not the only lever. Fewer people needing admission, shorter stays and faster discharges all free up space. We ran each policy at a size that has been proposed or achieved somewhere. The effect of each on admissions and length of stay comes from measured data, not assumption: OHID attributable-admission estimates for smoking, a large cohort study for obesity, a test-negative study for flu vaccine effectiveness, NHS England's discharge data for delays, and HES readmission counts for readmissions (sources). 7. Not every hospital is the same

England's average hides a wide spread. Across acute trusts in , trusts at the same occupancy had very different long waits. Occupancy matters, but it is far from the whole story: case mix, local social care, A&E design and how wards are run all play a part.

8. The next ten years

What this analysis can and cannot tell you

  • Occupancy is robust. It follows mostly from the arithmetic of admissions, length of stay and beds, and both versions of the model agree.
  • Waits are uncertain. They depend on how flexibly beds can be used, which changed after the pandemic and is not directly measured. Read the two systems as a range.
  • Changes are more reliable than levels. Every scenario uses the same simulated patients as the baseline, so differences reflect the policy, not chance.
  • Demand responds to supply. More beds may let hospitals admit patients they now treat elsewhere, using up some of the gain.
  • Effects on waits are probably upper bounds. The simulator's waits rise and fall with occupancy several times more steeply than hospitals' do, and its winter peak in long waits is too sharp (further validation). So the cuts in long waits it shows for more beds or shorter stays are likely too large. Its effects on occupancy are much better founded: with each year's own beds, admissions, overnight stays and same-day discharges it reproduces past occupancy to within about 2 points.
  • The blame tests are associations. The trust and area comparisons in section 3 compare hospitals and months, not randomised changes, so a busier hospital can have both more delays and more waits for reasons of its own. The split of the rise in long waits relies on the simulator, and "flexibility" is inferred from waits rather than measured.
  • The model fails some tests. It does not reproduce the weekly pattern of occupancy, or why some trusts wait so much longer than others. These failures are published on the validation page.
