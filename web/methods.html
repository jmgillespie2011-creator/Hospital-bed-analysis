Methods

This page describes exactly what the simulator does. The full source code is in the GitHub repository; the model itself is a single file, js/model.js, which is the same code your browser runs and the code used for calibration.

In one paragraph. The model simulates individual (synthetic) patients arriving at English general & acute hospital beds over a year: emergency patients arriving through the day and night with seasonal and weekly patterns, and planned patients arriving on weekdays. Each patient needs a bed for a random length of time, some stay longer because their discharge is delayed after they are medically fit, and discharges only happen during the day. If no bed is free, emergency patients wait (the model's equivalent of a "trolley wait" in A&E after a decision to admit) and planned admissions are lost. Unknown quantities are tuned so that the model reproduces published 2024/25 figures, then you change the inputs and see what happens.

Why a simulation rather than a formula?

A simple ratio, occupancy = beds needed ÷ beds available, gives the average occupancy but says nothing about how often hospitals run out of beds. Patients do not arrive evenly: demand varies by hour, weekday and season, and lengths of stay vary enormously. Running out of beds happens on the busy days, so the average hides the problem. A discrete-event simulation reproduces that variation and counts the consequences: waits for a bed and lost planned admissions. The simple ratio is still shown as a check on the Validation page.

Model structure

Beds

  • Physical beds (B): general & acute beds open overnight in England (NHS England KH03 definition, which excludes maternity, mental health, learning disability and day-only beds).
  • Available beds = B × min(1, staffing%). A bed without nurses to staff it cannot be used. At 100% staffing every physical bed is available, which is the baseline assumption because KH03 counts beds that are open.
  • Winter escalation: an extra percentage of beds open from 1 December to 31 March.
  • Effective bed pools. In reality beds are divided between ~120 acute trusts, and within each trust between wards, specialties and single-sex bays. A patient cannot use a free bed in another hospital, and often not in another ward. The model represents this by splitting available beds into independent pools of size S; each simulation run models one pool with 1/(B/S) of national demand, and results are scaled back to England. Smaller pools mean less sharing, so more waiting at the same average occupancy. S is not observable, so it is calibrated (see below). Results are shown for two values: "today's system" (S fitted to 2024/25 waits) and a "more flexible system" (S = 1,000, which reproduces 2015/16's near-zero waits).

Emergency patients

  • Arrivals: a Poisson process whose rate in hour h of day d is 
    λ(d,h) = (A / 365 / pools) × season(d) × weekday(d) × hour(h) / 24
season(d) = 1 + a × cos(2π (day-of-year − 15) / 365)    (peak mid-January; a = 4% × winter-peak setting)
    where A is emergency admissions per year. The weekday profile is 1.10, 1.03, 1.00, 1.00, 1.02, 0.93, 0.92 (Mon to Sun) and the hour-of-day profile peaks at midday-afternoon (both normalised to average 1; see HOURLY and WEEKDAY in the code). These shapes are assumptions informed by typical A&E patterns.
  • Same-day patients: a share (default 30%) leave within 3 to 10 hours (same day emergency care) and are never in a bed at midnight.
  • Length of stay for the rest: lognormal, LOS = median × mage × mcondition × (1 + LOS change) × eσZ with σ = 1.0 (a long right tail: most stays are a few days, a few last weeks). The median is calibrated.
  • Delayed discharge: once medically fit, a share of patients (default 20%, multiplied by an age factor) wait a further Gamma(shape 1.5)-distributed time before leaving (for social care, community beds, transport, etc.). The mean delay is calibrated to NHS England's count of patients who no longer meet the criteria to reside.
  • Discharge timing: discharges only happen within the discharge window (default 10:00–20:00); a patient fit to leave at night leaves the next day. At weekends only the chosen share (default 50%) are discharged; the rest wait until Monday.
  • No bed free: the patient joins a first-come-first-served queue and is admitted when a bed frees. Waiting time from arrival to bed is the model's equivalent of NHS England's "decision to admit to admission" wait.

Corridor care

Corridor care appears in two ways in the model, and the Model page reports both at 08:00 each day (averaged over the year, and the worst morning):

  • Waiting in A&E for a bed: emergency patients with a decision to admit but no free bed. They queue first-come-first-served; their waiting time gives the ">4h" and ">12h" figures (NHS England's decision-to-admit measure).
  • In temporary escalation spaces: if the temporary escalation spaces setting is above zero, patients waiting for a bed are placed in those spaces first and moved into a proper bed when one becomes free. They are no longer counted as waiting in A&E.

Neither group is counted in bed occupancy, which, like KH03, counts only patients in proper beds. The 2024/25 calibration targets the decision-to-admit waits, with no temporary spaces. This treats today's corridor care as part of the A&E wait, because there is no national time series that separates the two.

Planned (elective) overnight patients

  • Arrive on weekdays between 11:00 and 15:00 (Poisson). Length of stay lognormal, median 2 days, σ = 0.8.
  • If no bed is free they wait behind emergency patients; if still without a bed at 20:00, the admission is lost. In reality hospitals plan fewer operations when beds are short, rather than cancelling on the day, so read the "planned admissions lost" figure as planned capacity lost.

How each setting works

The settings are grouped into things policy can change (capacity, public health, hospital flow) and context that it cannot directly change (population, winter severity). Each one changes a specific model input; the "What the model is running" table on the Model page shows the resulting inputs, so you can check every effect.

Hospital capacity

  • General & acute beds: physical beds B.
  • Nursing posts filled: available beds = B × min(1, filled%). Extra beds therefore only help if they can be staffed. The model does not include the evidence that low staffing also lengthens stays and worsens outcomes, so staffing effects are, if anything, understated.
  • Extra winter beds: additional % of available beds from 1 December to 31 March.
  • Temporary escalation spaces (corridor care): extra trolleys or chairs used only when every proper bed is full. Emergency patients placed there count as admitted (their A&E wait ends) but not as occupying a G&A bed, matching how KH03 counts beds. Planned patients are never placed in them.
  • Planned surgery & treatment: scales the number of planned overnight admissions.

Public health

Risk factors change the number of people who need admitting. For a risk factor with prevalence p and relative risk of admission RR (people with the risk factor compared with people without), the population attributable fraction is the standard epidemiological formula

PAF = p (RR − 1) / (1 + p (RR − 1))

and changing prevalence from p₀ to p₁ multiplies admissions by

(1 + p₁ (RR − 1)) / (1 + p₀ (RR − 1))

RR is taken from published research or derived from a published count of attributable admissions (the sources are on the Data & sources page). Several risk factors are combined by multiplying their effects, which assumes they act independently. The effect is applied to both emergency and planned admissions.

Long-run effects. These are the effects once a change in prevalence has fully worked through. In reality the benefits of stopping smoking or losing weight build up over years (an ex-smoker's risk falls gradually), so these settings describe where a policy leads, not what happens the year it starts.
  • Adult smoking rate and adult obesity rate use the prevalence formula above.
  • Alcohol harm changes alcohol-related admissions (OHID's "narrow" measure, where alcohol is the main reason for admission) by the chosen %. Admissions change by share × change, where share is alcohol-related admissions as a share of all admissions.
  • Flu vaccination uptake (65+): influenza admissions are simulated as a separate winter wave (a bell-shaped curve peaking in early January), sized so the model reproduces the observed peak number of flu patients in G&A beds. Vaccination protects the people vaccinated, with vaccine effectiveness VE against hospital admission. With a share s of flu admissions in people aged 65+, changing uptake from u₀ to u₁ multiplies flu admissions by 
    s × (1 − VE·u₁)/(1 − VE·u₀) + (1 − s)
    This counts only direct protection. Vaccination also reduces spread to others, so the real benefit is likely to be larger. Vaccine effectiveness varies a lot from season to season with how well the vaccine matches circulating strains. The "severity of the flu season" setting scales the size of the wave independently of vaccination.
  • Close the deprivation gap: people in more deprived areas are admitted more often. Each fifth of the population (by Index of Multiple Deprivation) has a relative admission rate rq. Closing the gap by g moves each fifth towards the least deprived: rq' = rmin + (rq − rmin)(1 − g). Admissions change by Σ rq' / Σ rq. The gap partly reflects risk factors, so combining this with smoking and obesity changes double-counts some of the effect.

Hospital flow

  • Same day emergency care: share of emergency patients who leave within hours and never occupy a bed overnight.
  • Delayed discharges: scales the average time medically fit patients wait to leave (calibrated to NHS England's count of patients who no longer meet the criteria to reside). Represents social care, care home and community bed capacity.
  • 7-day discharging: share of patients ready to leave at the weekend who leave then; the rest wait until Monday.
  • Earlier discharge in the day: start of the daily discharge window (default 10:00; ends 20:00). Patients ready overnight leave when the window opens.
  • Virtual wards / hospital at home: shortens the bed stay of patients aged 75+ by the chosen share.
  • Community urgent response for older people: removes the chosen share of emergency admissions in people aged 75+.
  • Length of stay programmes: changes the clinically needed length of stay for all patients.

Population (context)

Emergency demand is built up by age band:

admissions(band, year) = admissions(band, base year) × population(band, year) / population(band, base year)

so admission rates per head are held at base-year levels and only the size and age structure of the population change. Each band has its own length-of-stay and delayed-discharge multipliers (older patients stay longer and are more often delayed). Population comes from ONS mid-year estimates and projections.

Randomness and accuracy

Each result is the average of many simulated years (30 for "today's system", 6 for the "more flexible system", whose larger pools are less noisy). The same random seeds are used for the baseline and your scenario, so differences between them reflect your changes rather than chance. Small differences (under about half a percentage point of occupancy) should not be over-interpreted.

Main limitations

  • The pool-size abstraction. No single pool size reproduces both 2015/16 and 2024/25. Hospitals now generate far longer waits at a given occupancy than a decade ago (see Validation), which is why two systems are shown. Occupancy results barely depend on this; wait results depend on it a lot.
  • Demand responds to supply in reality. With more beds, hospitals may admit patients they currently treat elsewhere, which would use some of the extra capacity. The model holds admission rates fixed unless you change them.
  • National, not local. All trusts are treated as identical. Real pressure is uneven: some trusts are far fuller than average.
  • Simplified patient flow. No transfers between wards, no critical care, no outlying, no paediatric/adult split, no infection-control bed closures.
  • Assumed shapes. Hourly and weekday arrival profiles, the lognormal LOS shape and the delay distribution are assumptions; calibration ensures the totals match, not every detail.
