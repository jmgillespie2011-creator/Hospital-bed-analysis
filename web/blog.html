Why England's hospitals are full, and why the waits exploded after 2020

England's hospitals run at about 92% full, with fewer general and acute beds per person than ever. Waits in A&E were already lengthening before the pandemic. Since 2022 the same fullness has produced several times as many 12-hour waits in A&E. We put three decades of published NHS data together with a simulation of every emergency and planned admission to find out why, who is to blame, and what would help.

In brief.

1. A system with no slack

NHS England counts general & acute (G&A) beds open overnight in its KH03 return. On a consistent definition, England had such beds in 1987/88 and has today. Over the same period the number of people aged 75 and over, who account for nearly half of emergency bed days, grew from to .

By 2019 hospitals were running above 90% full with little room to absorb a bad week. That made them fragile. It does not explain what happened next.

2. After 2020: the same fullness, far longer waits

A full hospital makes patients wait. When no bed is free, the patient stays in A&E, sometimes on a trolley in a corridor, until one is. NHS England counts these waits in two ways. Its monthly figures start the clock when a doctor decides to admit: on that measure 12-hour waits happened to , and to in , the year the simulator is set up for. Before 2020 they were almost unheard of. But that measure exaggerates the change, because before the pandemic the decision to admit was often recorded late. Hospital records also time each patient's whole stay in A&E from arrival, and on that clock long waits were common before the pandemic too.

Counted from arrival, patients spent over 12 hours in A&E in 2018/19, against counted from the decision to admit. From arrival, 12-hour waits have risen about -fold since then, not -fold. Other measures that do not depend on when a decision is recorded tell the same story at the same occupancy: 4-hour waits counted from arrival roughly doubled, and ambulance handover delays rose about fourfold. The real change is a several-fold rise in long waits at the same level of fullness.

So something about how hospitals work changed. The next section tests the four usual suspects.

3. What broke? Beds, social care, GPs, or how hospitals run

Four explanations dominate the debate. There are simply too few beds. Social care cannot take patients who are ready to leave, so they block beds. GPs are too hard to see, so patients end up in A&E and then in hospital. Or hospitals themselves manage the flow of patients badly. We tested each against published data, then asked the simulator how much each could matter. No single test proves cause, but together they point one way.

What changed between and today

Start with what actually changed. The simulator reruns and switches its measured conditions to today's one at a time: how many patients arrive, how many beds there are, how long overnight patients stay (including waiting to be discharged), how many go home the same day, and how flexibly beds are used. Averaging over every order of switching splits the change exactly between the five.

Some hospitals lost far more flexibility than others

England's average hides a wide spread. Across acute trusts in , trusts at the same occupancy had very different long waits.

Are those differences new? One clue looked strong. Group acute trusts into fifths by the share of their beds held by patients in hospital three weeks or more. Since the pandemic, the fifth with the fewest long-stay patients has had of emergency admissions waiting over 12 hours for a bed, and the fifth with the most , at about the same occupancy (). On that measure the gap looks new, because before the pandemic every group had almost no 12-hour waits. But that is the recording problem from section 2 again: the decision-to-admit measure was near zero everywhere before 2020. On the arrival clock, waits rose about equally in every group, from of patients over 4 hours in A&E before the pandemic to after, whatever the long-stay share. Comparing each trust with itself, each standard deviation of long-stay share predicts a bigger rise in 12-hour waits from the decision to admit () but not in 4-hour waits from arrival ().

So long-stay patients go with longer waits for a bed today, but there is no sign that they explain why waits broke. The 4-hour measure mostly covers patients who are never admitted, so it is a blunt test of anything that blocks beds. But the other long-stay evidence points the same way: long stays grew only a little, they move with long waits but do not lead them, and in a simulation of a hospital split into specialty wards, blocked beds are too small an effect to matter.

The verdict

If the break is mostly about flow, the fixes are about flow too. But hospitals that run this full need room as well.

4. What would help

There are two kinds of fix: more room, and better use of the room there is. The simulator runs a full year of patients through England's G&A beds, with numbers set from published data for , so both can be compared on the same footing.

Needing fewer beds

Fewer people needing admission, shorter stays and faster discharges all free up space. We ran each policy at a size that has been proposed or achieved somewhere. The effect of each on admissions and length of stay comes from measured data, not assumption: OHID attributable-admission estimates for smoking, a large cohort study for obesity, a test-negative study for flu vaccine effectiveness, NHS England's discharge data for delays, and HES readmission counts for readmissions (sources). 5. The next ten years

What this analysis can and cannot tell you

  • Occupancy is the firmer result. It follows mostly from the arithmetic of admissions, length of stay and beds, and with each year's own beds, admissions, overnight stays and same-day discharges the model reproduces past occupancy to within about 2 points.
  • Effects on waits are probably upper bounds. They depend on how flexibly beds can be used, which is inferred, not measured, and the simulator's waits rise and fall with occupancy several times more steeply than hospitals' do (further validation). Read the two systems as a range, and the cuts in long waits as the most that could be expected.
  • Changes are more reliable than levels. Every scenario uses the same simulated patients as the baseline, so differences reflect the policy, not chance.
  • Demand responds to supply. More beds may let hospitals admit patients they now treat elsewhere, using up some of the gain.
  • The blame tests are associations. The trust and area comparisons compare hospitals and months, not randomised changes, so a busier hospital can have both more delays and more waits for reasons of its own. The split of the rise in long waits relies on the simulator.
  • The national model treats every trust alike. Fitting each trust its own flexibility ranks trusts' later waits well but predicts their level only roughly. These and other failures are published on the validation page.
