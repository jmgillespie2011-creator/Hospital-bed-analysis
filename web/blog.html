Where the beds went: England's hospitals at 92% full

England's hospitals have fewer general and acute beds than at almost any time on record. They also serve a population that is larger and much older than it was. We put three decades of published NHS data together with a simulation of every emergency and planned admission. The aim was to show what fullness costs, why that cost rose sharply after the pandemic, and which policies would actually free up space.

1. Forty years of fewer beds

NHS England counts general & acute (G&A) beds open overnight in its KH03 return. The way beds were counted changed in 2010/11. We bridge the two series using the one year both methods cover. 2. Full hospitals, and the break after the pandemic

A full hospital makes patients wait. When no bed is free, the patient stays in A&E, sometimes on a trolley in a corridor, until one is. NHS England publishes the number who wait more than 12 hours from the decision to admit them. Before 2020 that was a rare event. Today it happens to about of emergency admissions.

The striking thing is not that waits rise as hospitals fill. It is that the same occupancy now produces far longer waits than it did before the pandemic. Each dot below is one quarter. Before 2020, quarters at 90–93% occupancy had almost no 12-hour waits (under 0.3% of admissions). Since 2022, quarters at the same occupancy have had 5–11% of admissions waiting more than 12 hours.

Why? The published data cannot say for certain. Patients are older and sicker, and more of them are waiting to be discharged. Beds may also be less interchangeable than before, through more single-specialty wards and infection-control rules. In the simulator, that last explanation is what fits the data. Today's waits are reproduced only if hospitals behave as if their beds are split into small pools that cannot lend to each other. The waits of 2018/19 are reproduced only if the pools are large and flexible. We show both versions throughout, as "today's system" and "a more flexible system". The validation page sets out the evidence.

4. Where else could the space come from?

Beds are not the only lever. Fewer people needing admission, shorter stays and faster discharges all free up space. We ran each policy at a size that has been proposed or achieved somewhere. The effect of each on admissions and length of stay comes from measured data, not assumption: OHID attributable-admission estimates for smoking, a large cohort study for obesity, a test-negative study for flu vaccine effectiveness, NHS England's discharge data for delays, and HES readmission counts for readmissions (sources). 6. Not every hospital is the same

England's average hides a wide spread. Across acute trusts in , trusts at the same occupancy had very different long waits. Occupancy matters, but it is far from the whole story: case mix, local social care, A&E design and how wards are run all play a part.

7. The next ten years

What this analysis can and cannot tell you

  • Occupancy is robust. It follows mostly from the arithmetic of admissions, length of stay and beds, and both versions of the model agree.
  • Waits are uncertain. They depend on how flexibly beds can be used, which changed after the pandemic and is not directly measured. Read the two systems as a range.
  • Changes are more reliable than levels. Every scenario uses the same simulated patients as the baseline, so differences reflect the policy, not chance.
  • Demand responds to supply. More beds may let hospitals admit patients they now treat elsewhere, using up some of the gain.
  • The model fails some tests. It does not reproduce the weekly pattern of occupancy, or why some trusts wait so much longer than others. These failures are published on the validation page.
